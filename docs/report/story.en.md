## The question

**Status: candidate.** Chosen on 2020–2023 (in sample for the choice), shown on 2024 seen by the developers; the test on held-out data, 2025, is pending. Can a forecast of Québec's hourly electricity demand be trusted, and what would show that it should stop being trusted? This project does not look for the best forecast: it checks this one the way a model-risk team would. It measures its error on months it has not seen, checks what its ranges are worth, then replays whole years as in production, under a monitor that can recommend investigating the data, recalibrating the ranges or retraining the model. The monitor recommends; it never acts. ([section 1](validation.en.md#1-the-methods-in-plain-words))

## The forecast

At the end of each day, v2 forecasts the next day's 24 hours from a temperature forecast: that of NOAA's National Blend of Models for Montréal's airport, already issued at that time, no longer the day before's temperature as in v1. That one point stands for the whole province. v2 turns this forecast into demand through one temperature curve for each hour of the day, beside the hour, the day type and the demand of the day before and of the week before. Each month from 2020 to 2023 is forecast by a model fitted on the hours before it only. Over the 34,997 hours where the three forecasts exist, the mean error is 2.83% for v2, 3.48% for v1's form on the same forecast and 4.42% for v1 as shipped. These years served to choose v2: v2's advantage there is biased upward by that choice, and it does not prove v2 better. With the temperature actually observed at the target hour, as if the weather were perfectly forecast, v2's mean error would be 2.70%: an optimistic bound the forecast does not have. ([section 2](validation.en.md#2-the-forecast-input), [section 3](validation.en.md#3-validation-on-20202023-in-sample-for-the-choice-of-family-and-input))

## Where it breaks: the cold

On 2020–2023 (_in sample for the choice of family and input_), its mean error runs from 2.13% in summer to 3.56% in winter; by decile of forecast load (ten bands, from the lowest to the highest), it runs from 1.98% (decile 1) to 3.72% (decile 9). At the 5 busiest hours of 2019–2023, all during the 2023-02 cold peak, demand stayed inside the 90% range at 2 of the 5 hours judged; it went above it at 3. At the coldest hours, those whose temperature forecast is below −12.0 °C, v2's error is smaller than v1 as shipped's and smaller than v1's form's, on the years of the choice; only one peak is studied, and a smaller gap at the busiest and coldest hours was one of the criteria v2 was chosen on: an advantage there is an outcome of that choice, not evidence for it. ([section 3](validation.en.md#3-validation-on-20202023-in-sample-for-the-choice-of-family-and-input), [section 4](validation.en.md#4-test-cases))

![Cold peak](figures/validation/validation-peak-hours.en.png)

The curves themselves were read and checked after the freeze, deciding nothing. The tolerances of those checks were fixed after the developers had seen the curves of the model frozen for 2024. The heating base is v1's, fixed before any v2 curve existed. Given the day before's demand, the curve's average slope from −20 to −5 °C is 306 to 385 MW per °C colder, depending on the hour. The curves are not straight lines, so one degree can add more or less than this average. Without the lags, the average slope is 427 to 536 MW per °C. Beside it stands the 500 MW per degree a Hydro-Québec spokesperson quoted, a figure whose definition is not given. 5 checks fail: findings, which change neither the model nor the 2025 verdict; the curves are lowest between 14 and 19 °C depending on the hour, whereas v1's base is 18 °C. ([section 3.1](validation.en.md#31-conceptual-soundness-added-after-the-freeze))

## How much confidence does it deserve?

Each forecast comes with a range that aims to hold demand 90% of the time, its nominal level; nothing guarantees that it does, so its coverage is measured. From 2021-01 to 2023-12 (_in sample for the choice of family and input_), v2's held it 90.3% of the time: the gap to the nominal stays within the uncertainty of the measurement. It falls short where demand leaves it more often than the nominal allows, beyond that uncertainty: winter; hour block day (10–15 h); forecast-load deciles 7, 8, 9 and 10. It is wider than needed where demand stays inside more often than the nominal, beyond that uncertainty: summer and fall; hour blocks night (0–5 h) and late evening (21–23 h); forecast-load deciles 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. The coverage thresholds of winter are drawn from weeks that already had this defect: the monitor does not flag it as a degradation. Coverage is never a criterion of the status. ([section 5](validation.en.md#5-prediction-intervals), [section 10](validation.en.md#10-limitations))

## 2024, replayed but already seen

The year 2024 was replayed hour by hour, as in production. The model, frozen on the data up to 2023-12-24, was not refitted, and every read, temperature forecasts included, went through a clock that refuses any later data. v2's mean error was 2.58%, and its 90% range held demand 92.2% of the time over the year. Week by week, it held demand less often than the nominal in 10 of the 13 winter weeks judged. These weekly shares carry no interval and decide nothing. The monitor recommended nothing all year. Against v1 as shipped, the verdict is "better"; against v1's form on the same forecast, "better". But 2024 is not a test: the developers had seen it through v1 before choosing v2. A success on a year already seen is weak evidence, which supports no claim. ([section 8](validation.en.md#8-2024-replayed-seen-by-the-developers))

## Is silence good news?

A silent monitor reassures only if it can see a real change. The report measured that by imposing sustained changes at a few fixed starts and replaying the real rules. A bias is injected into the forecast itself, on top of its own errors. On 2024 (seen by the developers), a sustained bias injected into the forecast, at each size tested from ±4% to ±8%, yielded "retrain" within 8 weeks at each of the 4 starts; a bias of ±2% did at 4 of 8 cases. The forecast leans on the day before's demand: it follows part of a lasting rise in demand. The gap the rise opens between the two is therefore smaller than the rise. A sustained 10% rise yielded "retrain" within 10 weeks at 4 of 4 starts on 2023 (the rehearsal year), and at 4 of 4 on 2024 (seen by the developers). Silence is therefore no proof: sustained changes went unnoticed at some of the starts measured. ([section 7](validation.en.md#7-the-frozen-model-rehearsed-on-2023-rehearsal-on-a-design-year), [section 8](validation.en.md#8-2024-replayed-seen-by-the-developers))

## The honesty ledger

What changed since v1: the temperature input, a forecast instead of the day before, and the model, one curve per hour. Both were chosen on 2020–2023, after the developers had seen v1's errors on 2024, its departure and a preliminary trial of six model families on those same years; at that time nobody had computed a v2 model on 2024 or read any demand of 2025. That is why 2020–2023 and 2024 prove nothing: only 2025 can. **Pending:** 2025 will judge, on held-out data, the verdicts against v1 as shipped and against v1's form on the same forecast, the criteria that could still show v2 wrong; they stay pending as long as those data do not exist. v2's rules were frozen (tag `rules-frozen-v2`, commit `825b7bd134a8`) before v2's 2024 replay, and no departure has changed them since. What backs that order is the annotated tag, unsigned, whose date the developers' own tools recorded, and the start times the replay ledger records: nothing outside the repository anchors it. Every 2024 or 2025 replay finished since the repository's history was rewritten is entered in the replay ledger, [`docs/replay-ledger.json`](../replay-ledger.json) (the 2023 rehearsal is not), and only v2's entries carry an evaluation label: an entry is never rewritten, and a replay that does not match its entry makes the report refuse. ([section 8](validation.en.md#8-2024-replayed-seen-by-the-developers), [section 9](validation.en.md#9-2025-the-test-of-record))

What the report does not show: an independent human review of the code (everything comes from AI agents under the direction of a single owner), the soundness of NOAA's model itself, the drift false-alarm rate in 2024, or the forecasts' real arrival delays, which are assumed. Other limits hold throughout. One forecast point, Montréal's airport, stands for the whole province. NOAA's model changes over the years are not tracked, so the forecast series is not homogeneous. No revision of any source is modelled. The day before's demand is taken as known when the forecast is issued, with the value the download holds and no publication delay. ([section 10](validation.en.md#10-limitations))
